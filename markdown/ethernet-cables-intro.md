# An Introduction to Ethernet Cables

## A Brief History

Ethernet was developed at Xerox PARC in 1973 by Robert Metcalfe and David Boggs, first as a way to connect computers to a shared laser printer. The earliest versions ran over thick coaxial cable ("Thicknet," 10BASE5) and later thinner coax ("Thinnet," 10BASE2). In 1980, Digital Equipment Corporation, Intel, and Xerox published the DIX standard, and in 1983 the IEEE formalized Ethernet as IEEE 802.3. The big shift came in 1990 with 10BASE-T, which carried Ethernet over inexpensive twisted-pair copper wiring, similar to telephone cable, with RJ45 connectors. Since then, speeds have climbed from 10 Mbps to 100 Mbps (Fast Ethernet), 1 Gbps (Gigabit Ethernet), 10 Gbps, and beyond. The cable categories (Cat 3, Cat 5, Cat 5e, Cat 6, and later) were developed alongside to support those higher speeds.

## What They're Used For

Ethernet cables make wired connections between devices on a local area network (LAN). Typical uses include connecting computers, game consoles, smart TVs, and printers to a router or switch; linking switches, routers, and servers in offices and data centres; and connecting network devices such as IP cameras and wireless access points. Compared with Wi-Fi, a wired connection is usually faster, more stable, and lower in latency, and it is less affected by interference. It can also be more secure, because someone needs physical access to the network to tap into it. With Power over Ethernet (PoE), one cable can carry both data and electrical power, so devices like VoIP phones, security cameras, and access points don't need a separate power supply.

## Naming Conventions

Ethernet cabling uses two naming systems. The first is the **category** ("Cat") rating, which describes the cable's electrical performance: **Cat 5e** supports up to 1 Gbps at 100 MHz, **Cat 6** supports 1 Gbps (10 Gbps up to about 55 m) at 250 MHz, **Cat 6a** supports 10 Gbps at 500 MHz over the full 100 m, and **Cat 8** reaches 25–40 Gbps over short runs of about 30 m, mainly in data centres. The second system is the **IEEE standard name**, such as **10BASE-T**, **100BASE-TX**, **1000BASE-T**, or **10GBASE-T**. The number is the speed in Mbps (or Gbps, when a "G" is present). "BASE" means baseband signalling, where the whole cable carries a single signal. The suffix gives the medium: "T" for twisted pair, "F", "SR" or "LR" for fibre variants, and so on. Cables are also labelled by shielding: **UTP** (unshielded twisted pair), **STP/FTP** (shielded or foiled), and combinations such as **S/FTP**, where the letter before the slash describes the overall shield and the letters after it describe how each pair is shielded.

## Other Things Worth Knowing

Most Ethernet cables have four twisted pairs of copper wires, terminated with **RJ45** (technically 8P8C) connectors. The wires follow one of two colour-coded wiring standards, **T568A** or **T568B**. A cable with the same standard on both ends is a **straight-through** cable, used for most connections. A cable with T568A on one end and T568B on the other is a **crossover** cable, which was once needed to connect two similar devices directly. Most modern equipment supports **Auto-MDIX**, which detects the cable type automatically, so crossover cables are now largely unnecessary. Copper Ethernet has a maximum run length of **100 metres** (328 ft), usually split into 90 m of permanent cable plus up to 10 m of patch cords. Cables also come as **solid-core** or **stranded**. Solid-core cable is better for long runs installed in walls. Stranded cable is more flexible and suits short patch cables. For longer distances or very high speeds, **fibre-optic** Ethernet is used instead of copper. Finally, a network only runs as fast as its slowest part, so the cable, the network cards, and the switch or router all have to support the speed you want.
